# Augustinian Just War — The Discipline of Judging Force
## Track A of the two Augustinian warfare programs. The outward question: *is this use of force just?*

## Why This Exists

Augustine gave the West two different vocabularies for "warfare," and confusing them is one of the oldest errors in moral reasoning. One is **outward**: when, if ever, may a people take up force, and how must they wage it? The other is **inward**: the war between two loves in the human heart (`docs/31`). This document is the outward track.

The purpose is not to produce warriors or to validate whatever war a child's tribe happens to be fighting. The just-war criteria exist **mainly to say no** — to restrain, to disqualify, to refuse. A child trained here should be slower to fight, harder to manipulate into fighting, and able to refuse an unjust fight *with reasons*.

The skill transfers all the way down: the same checklist that judges a nation's war also judges a playground escalation, an online pile-on, and an institutional crackdown. Force is force; the questions are the same.

---

## The Lineage

- **Augustine of Hippo** (*Contra Faustum* XXII; *City of God* XIX): peace is the goal even of war; force is tragic and permissible only under strict conditions and right intention. War is never loved; at most it is endured to restore a just peace.
- **Thomas Aquinas** (*Summa Theologiae* II-II, q.40): systematizes three conditions — legitimate authority, just cause, right intention.
- **The Catholic tradition** (*Catechism of the Catholic Church* §2309): the modern formulation of the conditions for legitimate defense by military force.
- **Secular parallel**: International Humanitarian Law — the Hague and Geneva Conventions — encodes the same logic (distinction, proportionality, prohibited means) in civil law. Teaching both shows the child the principles are not sectarian; they are how serious people across traditions constrain violence.

## The Checklist

### Jus ad bellum — *may we resort to force at all?*

1. **Just cause.** A real, grave, lasting harm — defense against aggression, protection of the innocent. Not pride, revenge, territory, or gain.
2. **Legitimate / competent authority.** The right person or body decides — not a self-appointed avenger or a mob.
3. **Right intention.** The aim is a just peace and the restoration of justice, not domination, humiliation, or hatred.
4. **Proportionality.** The good expected must outweigh the evils and disorders the force will produce.
5. **Probability of success.** Futile destruction is not justified; hopeless force only multiplies suffering.
6. **Last resort.** Every peaceful means has been genuinely tried or seriously considered and found wanting.

### Jus in bello — *how may force be waged?*

- **Discrimination / distinction.** Combatants may be targeted; noncombatants may not. Deliberately targeting the innocent is never permitted.
- **Proportionality of means.** Use no more force than the legitimate objective requires.
- **No intrinsically evil means.** Some acts — targeting innocents, torture, terror, mutilation — are wrong *in themselves*. A just cause never licenses them. This is the hinge: **a good end does not sanctify an evil means.**

---

## The Two Judgments Must Stay Separate

The most common error the program trains against: collapsing *jus ad bellum* and *jus in bello* into a single verdict. A side can have a **just cause** and still fight it by **unjust means** — and then that conduct is condemned regardless of how righteous the cause was. Conversely, a side with a weak cause is not thereby licensed to be slaughtered. The child must learn to render **two** verdicts, not one.

## How the Program Teaches It (Phases)

| Phase | Tier | What the child does |
|-------|------|---------------------|
| 1. The Rules of a Fair Fight | bronze | Learn the criteria as plain-language questions; apply to everyday conflicts, not armies. |
| 2. Judging Real Cases | silver | Apply the full checklist to documented historical conflicts and to a real online pile-on. |
| 3. Both Sides of the Table | gold | Steelman both sides before a verdict; separate a just cause from unjust means. |
| 4. Living It — Refusal & Restraint | platinum | Apply the standard to real conflicts the child personally faces; log the restrained response. |
| 5. Teach the Checklist | diamond | Transmit the method so others can judge a fresh case independently. |

The capstone is pedagogical, not performative: you have mastered the checklist when you can teach someone else to run it without you.

## Everyday Translations (for the 6–12 band)

- **Just cause** → "Was someone actually being hurt, or were you just mad?"
- **Legitimate authority** → "Was it your call to make, or someone else's?"
- **Right intention** → "Did you want things fixed, or did you want to win?"
- **Proportionality** → "Did the punishment fit, or did you blow it up?"
- **Last resort** → "Did you try talking first?"
- **Distinction** → "Did your response hit people who had nothing to do with it?"

## Guardrails

- This is **not** glorification of violence, enemy-lists, or eagerness for conflict. If it produces contempt for a group or appetite for a fight, it has failed.
- Every repeatable cycle must include at least one case where the child's **own** side/choice fails the test. A checklist that only ever absolves the home team is being gamed.
- Reward correct **refusals and restraint** at least as richly as verdicts of justification.
- Reject: verdicts scoring one side as perfect on every criterion; hypotheticals where a real case is required; strawmanned opponents.
- A verdict reached while a known disordered love (hatred, tribal pride, revenge) is active is suspect — see `docs/31`. The inner work is the integrity check on the outer judgment.
